Chapter 3 opens the Two Rivers to the world. The peddler brings war, a false Dragon who can channel, and Aes Sedai riding south, and the village turns his news into a shouting match of half-remembered legend. It is also the chapter where Perrin, Nynaeve and Egwene step on stage: the three boys learn they each saw the rider and each got a coin, and Egwene's new braid tells Rand the life he expected is already changing.

## Setting

The whole chapter happens in front of the Winespring Inn, but the world it talks about stretches from the Blight to the Sea of Storms. The village square becomes a stage for the outside.

| Place | What it shows | What it hides |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The peddler's wagon | Wheels taller than the people, pots clattering, a crowd begging for pins, lace, books and news | A stage for Fain's performance, and for the man himself |
| The Green before the inn | The whole village pressed together, then left muttering in small huddles | How quickly fear spreads once a word like "Dragon" is spoken |
| The inn door | Shut in the crowd's face while the Council questions Fain | The second closed meeting in two chapters; only Nynaeve walks in uninvited |
| The old stone foundation | Where Rand sits alone to think about the news | The ruin from Chapter 1, the trace of [WoT — Manetheren](../../world/manetheren.md), now a seat for a boy dreaming of the outside |
| The places in Fain's news | Ghealdan, the Dhallin Forest, Tar Valon, Illian, the Borderlands | Names Rand knows only from stories, now suddenly real and at war |

## Characters

Four important characters step on stage for the first time: Padan Fain, Perrin, Nynaeve and Egwene. Each arrives with a defining trait already fixed.

| Character | Mindset | Wants | Key moment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Padan Fain | Showman; always smiling "as if he knew a joke that no one else knew"; friendly from a distance | The crowd's full attention before he speaks | When Rand's shout breaks the silence, Fain gives him "a sharp, searching look" |
| Perrin Aybara | Careful and gentle: apologises as he moves through a crowd he could simply push aside; slow to anger | No war, no Aes Sedai in the Two Rivers | Reveals he too saw the rider, watching the smithy at dusk, and that Moiraine gave him a coin |
| Nynaeve al'Meara | Young, fierce, absolutely sure of her authority; a stick in her hand and a temper she uses | To keep fools from harming themselves, and to get the right answers from the peddler | Scolds Mat for taunting Bili Congar, then walks into the closed Council meeting: "It will take the Women's Circle to find out anything useful" |
| Egwene al'Vere | Grave, then teasing; restless | To see the places in the stories | Shows Rand her new braid, says she is training as Nynaeve's apprentice and may serve a village outside the Two Rivers |
| Rand | Torn: he would "rather have blizzards and wolves", yet imagines "a whole lifetime" of adventure | To understand the outside, and to keep Egwene from slipping away | Says "No one ever leaves the Two Rivers", and offends her |
| Mat | Excited, reckless, curious | To see the false Dragon | Tells the guard's story that the Dragon will save mankind; admits he goaded Bili Congar into naming the Dark One |
| Tam | Calm, open-minded | To steady the Council | Defends Ewin's question and says he never met anything that couldn't be talked about |

**Named but not on stage:** Mistress Ayellin, whose fever kept Nynaeve away; Bili Congar, who named the Dark One. The chapter ends as a white-haired man bursts out of the inn, the gleeman, still unnamed.

## The news from Outside

Fain sells the news like goods, each piece held back until the crowd begs for it. The village's answer is a chorus of half-remembered lore, and every voice believes something different.

| Fain says | The crowd hears |
| --- | --- |
| The winter, the late spring and the wolves are everywhere, not just here | Their private troubles are part of something larger |
| There is war in Ghealdan; the snows of the Dhallin Forest are red | A word, "war", that no one in the Two Rivers has had to use |
| "The standard of the Dragon has been raised" | The Dragon broke the world; the Dark One is loose; three false Dragons in five years means evil times |
| He doesn't know if this one is false | Cenn: look at the crops and the weather; maybe this one is real |
| "He can channel": the ground opens, walls fall, lightning strikes where he points | Ewin: men who channel go mad and die; Cenn: some things must not be spoken of |
| "A party of them has ridden south from Tar Valon" | Aes Sedai, the women the stories say broke the world, are now part of it |

Haral Luhhan, the blacksmith on the Council, is the one voice of precision: the Dragon is not the Dark One, and this is a false Dragon anyway. The Council closes the door before anyone can argue further.

## What the reader can't decode yet

The chapter's ironies land hardest on a second reading: almost every confident statement is wrong, or right for the wrong reason.

| Sign | What the village sees | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Fain's "sharp, searching look" at Rand | A peddler annoyed at being interrupted | The Shadow's agent noting the boy who stands out, the one the rider hunts |
| Fain's joke "no one else knew" | A showman's manner | He serves the Shadow and has led its hunters here |
| The three sightings and the three coins | Coincidence; Egwene says people do ride horses | Rider and Aes Sedai have narrowed the search to the same three young men |
| A false Dragon who can channel | A distant horror | The taint on saidin, and the madness Rand himself will fight |
| "The Dragon would be reborn ... and save us all" | A guard's foolish tale, laughed off | The prophecy is true, and the Dragon is sitting on the foundation stone |
| Rand: "As well ask for help from the Dark One" | Common sense | Spoken by the Dragon Reborn about himself |
| "A party of them has ridden south from Tar Valon" | Aes Sedai far away | One is already in Emond's Field, talking to the boys |
| The stories say Aes Sedai broke the world | Settled history | Half true: it was the male Aes Sedai, driven mad by the taint, who broke it |
| Egwene "can learn to listen to the wind" | A Wisdom's weather lore | A sign she can channel; the true Wisdoms who hear the wind are using the Power without knowing it |
| Bili Congar named the Dark One, then lost his crops to cutworm | Village superstition | In this world naming the Dark One draws his attention: a rule, not a superstition |
| Master Luhhan carried his biggest hammer after Perrin saw the rider | A blacksmith's caution | The first hammer in Perrin's story, long before he chooses one over his axe |

## Reader's notes

Your close-reading notes on Chapter 3, with what the later books make of each.

| Note | Reading |
| --- | --- |
| Fain "had taken notice of them" only the year before, "acknowledging them as men" | On the surface it is Rand's worry about being treated as a child. In hindsight it is ominous: Fain is the Dark One's hound, sent to find a young man of the right age, and the year the boys become men is the year they become suspects. |
| A false Dragon who can channel | Most false Dragons cannot channel; this one can, which is why Fain stresses it. Their rising number is a sign the true Dragon is near, a counterpart to the Dark One's stirring rather than its direct product. |
| The One Power named; men and women channelling | The village knows the stories about men who channel (madness, death) but is unsure about women; Aes Sedai are introduced through rumour. |
| Children of the Light | Named only in passing, as a second power people fear alongside the Aes Sedai. |
| Egwene "of a height with Nynaeve, and with the same dark coloring" | Hair and eyes, typical of Two Rivers folk; it also pairs apprentice and teacher, and quietly sets up Rand's different looks. |
| Rand dreams of adventure, then balks at Egwene leaving | Not a rule that only men ride out: "no one ever leaves" applies to everyone. What he fears is losing her, since a Wisdom serving another village would leave his life. |
| Three reactions to the news | Rand wonders what the outside world is like, Perrin worries, and Mat is excited. |

## Craft lessons

1. **Make exposition a performance.** Fain stages the news; the reader gets the world's politics through a showman with his own agenda, not a narrator's summary.
2. **Let lore arrive disputed.** The crowd's shouted beliefs contradict each other, so the reader learns what people fear, not yet what is true.
3. **Enter each character through one gesture.** Perrin apologises in a crowd he could push aside; Nynaeve walks through a door shut on everyone else; Egwene pulls her braid over her shoulder.
4. **Use the rule of three.** Three boys, three sightings, three coins: a pattern the reader notices before the characters do.
5. **Put the theme in a lovers' quarrel.** "No one ever leaves the Two Rivers" against "maybe I want to see the places in the stories" sets up the journey both of them will take.
6. **Stack ironies on the hero.** Rand dismisses the Dragon who saves, compares him to the Dark One, and sits on the ruins of Manetheren while doing it.
7. **Plant objects in the background.** A blacksmith's hammer carried at dusk is a detail now and a symbol later.
8. **End on an interruption.** Egwene's reply is cut off by a stranger bursting out of the inn, so the chapter stops mid-breath.
